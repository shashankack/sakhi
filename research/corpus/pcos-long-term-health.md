# PCOS and long-term health

## Plain-language explanation

WHO describes PCOS (now also called PMOS) as a **chronic** (long-term) condition that can continue beyond the years when someone can get pregnant. The NHS says symptoms usually start at puberty and continue until menopause.

PCOS is linked with a **higher risk** of some other health conditions. A higher risk does **not** mean these will happen. Regular check-ups help find problems early.

## PCOS education context

Long-term risks named by trusted sources:

- **Type 2 diabetes**: WHO links PCOS with insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and obesity. The NHS lists type 2 diabetes among the risks.
- **Heart and blood vessels**: WHO lists high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and cardiovascular disease. The 2023 international guideline says women with PCOS should be considered at **increased risk of cardiovascular disease**.
- **Liver**: the NHS lists non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. WHO also mentions a related liver condition.
- **Womb lining**: the 2023 guideline says premenopausal women with PCOS have a **markedly higher risk** of thickening of the womb lining (endometrial hyperplasia) and **womb cancer**. The NHS also lists womb cancer.
- **Mental health**: WHO lists anxiety, depression, eating disorders, and negative body image.
- **Sleep**: the 2023 guideline says obstructive sleep apnoea is more common in women with PCOS.
- **Pregnancy**: WHO lists gestational diabetes and high blood pressure in pregnancy. See `pregnancy-and-pcos.md`.

The NHS says **regular check-ups can reduce** the risk of these conditions.

This information is educational. It is not a diagnosis.

## Other possible causes and overlaps

These conditions are common in people **without** PCOS too. For example, the NHS says type 2 diabetes risk is higher for people over 25 with a South Asian background, people with a close family member with diabetes, and people who are overweight and not very active.

## Self-care notes

- WHO recommends **healthy eating and physical activity** for all women with PCOS, even without weight loss. See `indian-diet-pcos.md` and `exercise-and-yoga-pcos.md`.
- **Keep follow-up appointments** and ask which checks you need.
- If periods are often months apart, mention this to a clinician. See `cycles-irregular.md`.
- Look after sleep and mood. See `sleep-stress-pcos.md` and `body-image-and-emotional-health.md`.

## Care pathway

Checks that sources describe:

- WHO says women newly diagnosed with PCOS should have **blood tests for insulin resistance and heart risk**.
- The 2023 guideline says blood sugar status should be **rechecked every one to three years**, depending on other risk factors.
- The guideline says women with PCOS should be asked about **symptoms of sleep apnoea**, and screened for depression.

- **Routine**: regular check-ups with a GP, gynaecologist, or endocrinologist.
- **Soon**: feeling very thirsty, peeing more than usual, or losing weight without trying; loud snoring with breathing pauses; missed 3 periods in a row.
- **Urgent**: chest pain, or severe difficulty breathing. See `red-flags-when-urgent.md`.

## Questions for a clinician

- Which long-term health checks do I need, and how often?
- Should my blood sugar, blood pressure, and cholesterol be checked?
- Is my period pattern a risk for my womb lining?
- Could I have sleep apnoea?
- What can I do now to lower my long-term risks?
