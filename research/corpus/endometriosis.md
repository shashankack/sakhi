# Endometriosis

## Plain-language explanation

The NHS says **endometriosis** is where cells similar to those in the lining of the womb grow in **other parts of the body**. It can affect anyone who has or had a womb, and is usually diagnosed between puberty and menopause.

WHO says endometriosis affects an estimated **10% (190 million)** of reproductive-age women worldwide.

The NHS lists these symptoms:

- **severe period pain** that stops you from doing your normal activities
- **heavy periods**
- pain in the lower tummy and back (pelvic area)
- **pain when you poo or pee**
- **pain during or after sex**
- extreme tiredness (fatigue)
- difficulty getting pregnant

## PCOS education context

Endometriosis and PCOS (now also called PMOS) are **different conditions**. Both can affect periods and fertility, so they can be confused.

- WHO lists irregular or absent periods, extra hair growth, and acne among PCOS symptoms.
- The NHS lists **pain**, such as severe period pain, pelvic pain, and painful sex, among the main endometriosis symptoms.

A clinician looks at the full pattern of symptoms rather than one sign.

This information is educational. It is not a diagnosis.

## Other possible causes and overlaps

The NHS lists other conditions that can cause period pain:

- **adenomyosis**
- **fibroids**
- **pelvic inflammatory disease** (an infection)
- an **intrauterine device (IUD)**

See `painful-periods.md`.

**How it is found:** the NHS says diagnosis can take a long time. Tests can include an **ultrasound scan**, an **MRI scan**, and a **laparoscopy** (keyhole surgery). The NHS says a laparoscopy can confirm endometriosis.

## Self-care notes

- Keep a **symptom diary** for a few cycles: when pain happens, how bad it is, where it is, and whether it links to periods, sex, or going to the toilet.
- The NHS suggests heat, massage, gentle exercise, and pharmacy painkillers for period pain. Ask a pharmacist which painkiller is safe for you.
- Look after sleep and mood. Long-term pain can be tiring and upsetting. See `mood-fatigue.md`.

## Care pathway

The NHS advises seeing a GP if:

- you think you might have endometriosis
- symptoms affect your everyday life, work, or relationships
- symptoms do not improve with treatment, or get worse

- **Soon**: period pain that stops daily activities; pain during sex or when going to the toilet; painful periods with difficulty getting pregnant.
- **Urgent**: severe or worsening pelvic pain, or pelvic pain with heavy bleeding or fainting. See `red-flags-when-urgent.md`.

Who to see: a **GP** first, then a **gynaecologist**. The NHS says referral to a fertility specialist is possible if endometriosis affects getting pregnant.

## Questions for a clinician

- Could my symptoms be caused by endometriosis or adenomyosis?
- What tests would help, and what can a scan show or miss?
- What treatment options are there for pain?
- Could this affect my chances of getting pregnant?
- Should I be referred to a specialist?
