# Endometriosis

## Plain-language explanation

**Endometriosis** is a long-term condition where tissue similar to the lining of the womb grows **outside the womb**. It is often found on the ovaries, the tubes, or the lining of the pelvis.

This tissue reacts to monthly hormone changes. It can cause swelling, pain, and scar tissue. About **1 in 10** women and girls of reproductive age are thought to have it, but it often takes years to be recognised.

Common signs include:

- period pain that is **severe**, stops daily life, or gets worse over time
- pelvic pain **between periods**
- **pain during or after sex**
- pain when passing stools or urine, especially during periods
- feeling very tired
- **difficulty getting pregnant**
- sometimes heavy periods, bloating, or bowel upset around periods

Some people have very few symptoms. How bad the pain is does not always match how much tissue there is.

## PCOS education context

Endometriosis and PCOS are **different conditions**. They can be confused because both may affect periods and fertility.

- PCOS is more linked to **irregular or missing periods**, extra hair growth, and acne.
- Endometriosis is more linked to **pain**: painful periods, pelvic pain, and painful sex.

A person can have both. A clinician looks at the full pattern of symptoms rather than one sign.

This information is educational. It is not a diagnosis.

## Other possible causes and overlaps

Similar pain can come from:

- **Primary period pain** without another condition (see `painful-periods.md`)
- **Adenomyosis** (lining tissue inside the womb muscle)
- **Fibroids**
- **Pelvic infection**
- **Ovarian cysts**
- **Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)** or bladder problems
- **Ectopic pregnancy**, which is an emergency

There is no simple blood test for endometriosis. An ultrasound or MRI may show some types. Sometimes a small keyhole operation (laparoscopy) is used to confirm it. Clinicians may also suggest starting treatment based on symptoms.

## Self-care notes

- Keep a **symptom diary** for a few cycles: when pain happens, how bad (0–10), where it is, and if it links to periods, sex, or bowel movements.
- **Heat**, gentle stretching, and rest can ease pain for some people.
- Ask a pharmacist or doctor which **pain relief** is safe for you. Do not go above the packet instructions.
- Look after sleep and mood. Long-term pain can be tiring and upsetting, and support helps.
- Many people feel they were not believed for years. You can ask for a second opinion if your pain is not being taken seriously.

## Care pathway

- **Soon (within 1–2 weeks)**:
  - period pain that stops daily activities or keeps getting worse
  - pelvic pain between periods
  - pain during sex, or when passing stools or urine during periods
  - painful periods together with trouble getting pregnant
- **Routine (book in the next few weeks)**: milder symptoms that someone wants to understand.
- **Urgent (same day / emergency)**: sudden severe pelvic pain, pain with fever or fainting, or pain/bleeding with a possible pregnancy. See `red-flags-when-urgent.md`.

Who to see: a **GP** first, then a **gynaecologist**. Some cities have gynaecologists with special interest in endometriosis.

## Questions for a clinician

- Could my symptoms be caused by endometriosis or adenomyosis?
- What tests would help, and what can an ultrasound show or miss?
- What treatment options are there for pain, both hormonal and non-hormonal?
- Could this affect my chances of getting pregnant, and when should I plan for that?
- Is a referral to a specialist useful for me?
- What should I track before my next visit?
